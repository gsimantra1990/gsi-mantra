1. Shaping of the Earth's Surface

The Earth's surface is continuously changing. Both internal forces operating inside the Earth and external forces acting on its surface play an important role in shaping the Earth's surface.

Internal Forces

Internal forces originate from within the Earth. They can uplift, deform and create major landforms.

External Forces

External forces act on the Earth's surface and gradually modify existing landforms.

Weathering → Erosion → Transportation → Deposition
  • Weathering: Breakdown of rocks in their original position.
  • Erosion: Removal and wearing away of weathered material.
  • Transportation: Movement of sediments by water, wind, ice or gravity.
  • Deposition: Accumulation of transported sediments in a new location.

Interaction of Internal and External Forces

  • Internal forces generally build and elevate the Earth's surface.
  • External forces generally wear down, transport and redistribute materials.
  • The present landscape is the product of long-term geomorphic processes.

Diversity of Landforms

These processes produce a wide range of landforms, from high mountain peaks and deep valleys to plateaus, plains, river valleys, deltas, coastal features and deep ocean floors.

Importance of Landforms to Human Life

  • Climate and local environmental conditions
  • Availability of natural resources
  • Agricultural possibilities
  • Settlement patterns
  • Transportation routes
  • Economic activities
  • Cultural development

2. Earth's Interior

Crust

The crust is the outermost and thinnest layer of the Earth.

Mantle

The mantle lies below the crust and is much thicker. It contains very hot rocks and plays an important role in the movement of tectonic plates.

Lithosphere

  • The lithosphere consists of the crust and the upper part of the mantle.
  • It is divided into several tectonic plates.
  • The lithosphere is about 100 km thick.

Asthenosphere

  • The asthenosphere lies beneath the lithosphere.
  • It is a hot, mobile layer of partially molten rock.
  • It allows tectonic plates to move.

Core

  • Outer Core: A fluid layer mainly made of iron and nickel.
  • Inner Core: A solid, extremely hot and dense part of the Earth.

3. Tectonic Plates

Tectonic plates are large slabs of solid rock that move very slowly. They generally move a few centimetres per year.

Three Main Types

  • Continental plates: carry continents.
  • Oceanic plates: carry ocean floors.
  • Mixed plates: carry both continents and oceans.

Major plates include the Pacific, Eurasian, African, North American, South American, Indo-Australian, and Antarctic Plates.

Convection Currents

  • The movement of tectonic plates is mainly caused by convection currents in the mantle.
  • Heat from the Earth's core makes hot material rise, while cooler material sinks.
  • This continuous movement creates convection currents that push and pull tectonic plates.

4. Plate Boundaries

Boundary Movement Main Features / Example
Convergent Boundary Two plates move towards each other. Continental plates may form fold mountains, such as the Himalayas. An oceanic plate colliding with a continental plate can cause volcanic activity and earthquakes.
Divergent Boundary Two plates move away from each other. Magma rises and forms new crust. Example: Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
Transform Boundary Two plates slide past each other. Crust is neither created nor destroyed. This movement mainly causes earthquakes. Example: San Andreas Fault in the United States.

Importance of Plate Tectonics

  • Plate tectonics plays an important role in shaping the Earth's surface.
  • Plate movement forms mountains, valleys, ocean basins, volcanoes, and earthquakes.
  • It explains the distribution of continents and oceans.
  • Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along plate boundaries.

Ring of Fire

The Ring of Fire is an area around the Pacific Ocean. It has a high concentration of earthquakes and volcanoes. This is mainly due to the movement and interaction of tectonic plates.

5. Plate Tectonics, Earthquakes & Volcanoes

Earthquakes in India

India has experienced several major earthquakes in the past. Earthquakes can cause serious damage to human life, buildings, and the environment. Densely populated areas are especially vulnerable to earthquake damage.

Earthquakes in Ancient India

In ancient times, earthquakes were known as “Bhukampa”, meaning the shaking of the Earth.

Varahamihira discussed earthquakes in the Brihatsamhita. He connected earthquakes with observations of wind, rain, clouds, animal behaviour, and planetary alignments.

Mud Volcano

The Mud Volcano at Baratang Island is a rare natural feature of India. It is located in the Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Unlike a fiery volcano, it produces mud due to natural underground gases and pressure.

6. Weathering

Weathering is the process by which rocks on the Earth's surface break down into smaller pieces.

Important: Weathering does not involve the movement of broken material.

A. Physical Weathering

Rocks break into smaller pieces without changing their chemical composition.

B. Chemical Weathering

Chemical reactions change the minerals present in rocks.

C. Biological Weathering

Plants, animals and microorganisms can contribute to the breakdown of rocks.

7. Erosion, Transportation and Deposition

Erosion

Erosion is the removal and wearing away of weathered material.

Transportation

Transportation is the movement of sediments by water, wind, ice or gravity.

Deposition

Deposition is the accumulation of transported sediments in a new location.

Main Agents

  • Running water
  • Wind
  • Glaciers
  • Sea waves
  • Groundwater
  • Gravity

8. Glacial Landforms

Glaciers are large masses of ice that move slowly under their own weight. They erode, transport and deposit materials.

U-shaped Valley

A U-shaped valley is formed by glacial erosion.

Cirque

A cirque is a bowl-shaped depression associated with glacial activity.

Arete

An arete is a sharp ridge between glacial valleys or cirques.

Hanging Valley

A hanging valley is a smaller glacial valley situated above the main valley.

Fjord

A fjord is a deep, narrow sea inlet with steep sides formed from a glacial valley that has been flooded by seawater.

Moraines

Moraines are deposits of rocks, soil and debris carried and deposited by glaciers.

Type Formation
Lateral Moraine Forms along the sides of a glacier.
Terminal Moraine Forms at the end of a glacier.
Medial Moraine Forms in the middle when two glaciers meet.

9. Wind Erosion and Deposition

Wind Erosion

Wind erosion is especially important in dry and desert regions. Strong winds can carry loose sand, soil and dust.

Landforms of Wind Erosion

  • Yardangs: Streamlined ridges carved by wind erosion.
  • Ventifacts: Rocks polished and scratched by wind-blown sand.
  • Blowouts / Wind Hollows: Depressions formed when wind removes loose material.
  • Desert Pavements: Surfaces where coarse stones remain after finer particles are removed.

Sand Dunes

Sand dunes are hills or ridges of sand formed by wind deposition.

Type Characteristics
Barchan Crescent-shaped dunes.
Longitudinal Long narrow ridges generally parallel to prevailing winds.
Star Central peak with several arms.
Parabolic Generally U-shaped and commonly associated with vegetation.

10. Underground Water and Karst Landforms

In limestone regions, groundwater can dissolve rocks and create distinctive landforms known as karst landforms.

Karst Topography

Karst topography develops mainly where soluble rocks such as limestone are dissolved by slightly acidic groundwater.

  • Caves: Underground cavities formed by dissolution.
  • Stalactites: Deposits hanging from the cave roof.
  • Stalagmites: Deposits growing upward from the cave floor.
  • Pillars: Form when stalactites and stalagmites join.
  • Sinkholes: Depressions formed by dissolution or collapse.
  • Underground Rivers: Water channels flowing through caves.
Remember:
Stalactite → Ceiling
Stalagmite → Ground

11. Landforms and Human Life

  • Climate and local environmental conditions
  • Availability of natural resources
  • Agricultural possibilities
  • Settlement patterns
  • Transportation routes
  • Economic activities
  • Cultural development

Fertile plains generally support dense settlements and intensive agriculture, whereas steep mountainous regions often have limited settlement and transport possibilities.

12. Landforms and Natural Disasters

Understanding landforms is important for disaster preparedness and risk reduction. Areas with particular physical characteristics may be more vulnerable to earthquakes, landslides, avalanches, floods, volcanic eruptions, GLOFs and dust storms.

Landslides

A landslide is the downslope movement of soil, rock or debris under the influence of gravity.

Major Causes

  • Heavy rainfall
  • Earthquakes
  • Steep slopes
  • Weathering
  • Deforestation
  • Mining
  • Road construction
  • Unplanned construction

Avalanches

An avalanche is the sudden downward movement of snow and ice, sometimes mixed with rocks and debris, along a mountain slope.

Glacial Lake Outburst Flood – GLOF

A GLOF is a sudden release of water from a glacial lake because of the failure or overtopping of its natural dam.

Dust Storms

A dust storm occurs when strong winds lift large quantities of loose, dry soil and fine particles into the atmosphere.

14. Key Terms

  • Karst Topography: A distinctive landscape developed mainly in soluble rocks such as limestone through chemical dissolution by groundwater.
  • Sinkhole: A depression formed when the ground collapses into an underground cavity or when soluble rock is dissolved.
  • Landslide: The downslope movement of soil, rock or debris under the influence of gravity.
  • Avalanche: The sudden movement of snow and ice down a slope.
  • GLOF: Sudden release of water from a glacial lake.
  • Dust Storm: A storm in which strong winds carry large amounts of dust and fine soil.
